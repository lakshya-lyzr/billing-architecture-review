Recommendation: Autumn for billing; Pagos for credits.

Both Autumn and Lago Community handled our core subscription requirements locally. Autumn also handled bill previews, customer-specific prices and refund correction records that required Premium features or extra work in Lago Community.

The tradeoff: Autumn needs more infrastructure to run. It reduces the finance workflows we build, but the shared wallet, usage ledger and product SDKs remain our responsibility with either option.

Why we need this split

Today, billing-related responsibilities are spread across Pagos, product backends and Memberstack. The proposal moves subscription and payment work out of Memberstack, and gives every product one common way to use credits.

The customer experience

An organization can buy an Alpha plan and an Architect plan separately. Its users spend from one shared organization credit balance across both products.

Recurring charges are combined into one invoice for the agreed billing period. Buying extra credits or upgrading mid-cycle can create a separate immediate invoice.

The internal model

Pagos answers: “Can this task spend credits, and how much did it use?”

Autumn + Stripe answer: “Which plans were purchased, what should be invoiced, and was it paid?”

This is the agreed “Model C”: independent product plans, one organization payer and a shared credit wallet.

Proposed architecture

Products ask Pagos to spend. Billing runs in the background.

Every backend uses a thin SDK in its own language. The SDK calls the same Pagos API; it does not maintain a second wallet or reproduce billing rules.

Autumn and Stripe are outside the task approval path. Each task needs Pagos and its wallet database. A billing-provider delay should not block spending from already-funded credits; a Pagos wallet outage must fail safely instead of approving unrecorded spending.
Where locking, Redis and reliability fit

Atomicity belongs in Pagos’s PostgreSQL transaction. Check the spendable balance and reserve credits as one operation, using database locking or an equivalent conditional update. Two products cannot both spend the same available credits.

Redis is optional. It can cache display balances or help with rate limits. A cached balance must never authorize spending. The authoritative decision stays in the database.

Billing jobs are saved with the ledger change. A background worker delivers them and retries safely. Incoming payment events are recorded and deduplicated before applying credit changes. Regular reconciliation catches missing or mismatched records.

These are proposed production mechanisms. The new Pagos wallet’s throughput, concurrency and recovery behavior have not been implemented or load tested.

What happens when someone uses a product?

Example: a task may cost up to 10 credits

  1. Reserve before starting.The product asks Pagos to hold 10 credits. With a balance of 100, only 90 remain available for other tasks.
  2. Run the task if approved.Other products see the same reduced available balance. The SDK returns an insufficient-credit result if Pagos cannot reserve the amount.
  3. Report the actual usage.The product sends the operation ID, model, token counts and other usage facts. Suppose the final charge is 6 credits.
  4. Settle and release.Pagos spends 6, releases the unused 4 and records the transaction. The available balance becomes 94. Retrying the same settlement must not charge twice.

Illustrative amounts. Multi-step or open-ended tasks must extend their reservation before exceeding the approved amount. Failed or abandoned tasks need an explicit release or recovery policy.

The product reports facts; Pagos keeps the ledger

The ledger is the permanent record explaining every credit change: purchase, grant, reservation, spend, release, expiry or refund adjustment.

Pagos also records which product ran the task, which LLM model was used, input/output tokens and provider cost. What the provider costs us and what we charge the customer are separate fields.

Products provide stable operation and component IDs. Pagos uses them to prevent duplicate charges, including when two products report the same shared component.

Pagos saves the rate agreed for the operation so a price change during a task does not unexpectedly change its settlement.

A product can retain operational logs for debugging. It does not need to maintain its own authoritative billing ledger.

What happens when money moves?

  1. Buying a plan or extra credits: Autumn coordinates billing in Stripe. Pagos grants the agreed credits once it verifies the successful payment and applies the plan’s grant policy.
  2. Spending purchased credits: Pagos records consumption. This usage must not become another invoice charge: the credits were already funded.
  3. Explicit overage or extra fees: Pagos calculates the final monetary charge and adds it directly to the correct Stripe subscription’s next invoice. This adapter is code we own; it is not Autumn’s native usage metering.
  4. Refunding a payment: Autumn/Stripe handle the money and correction record. Pagos separately applies the agreed rule for reversing any associated credits.

Overage items must be attached to the subscription so a separate immediate top-up invoice does not pick them up. Our sandbox tests covered this separation and duplicate delivery.

Deployment details for the engineering team

Autumn: the tested stack included API, balance and queue workers, cron, event delivery and dashboard; PostgreSQL; cache/balance-store roles; Kafka; DynamoDB-compatible checkpoints; SQS-compatible queues; and EventBridge-compatible timers. Local queue/timer emulators and in-memory checkpoint storage are test substitutes, not a qualified production durability setup. Use durable services in our account or qualify replacements. Removing unused wallet infrastructure needs a separate investigation.

Lago: the basic PostgreSQL-backed event store does not require Kafka or ClickHouse. The tested deployment used API, worker, scheduler, frontend, PostgreSQL, Redis and a PDF service.

For either option, we own deployment automation, upgrades, alerts and recovery. Actual email delivery and full payment-page browser flows still need testing.

What is proven, and what is still work?

Validated locally

The billing approach fits the tested model

  • Independent product plans with combined recurring invoices.
  • Separate top-ups and immediate upgrade charges.
  • Annual/monthly billing, trials, discounts and negotiated prices.
  • Pagos-calculated overage, duplicate protection and late usage carried once to a later invoice.
  • Autumn previews, credit notes and refunds; selected payment-event replay and restart checks.
  • Autumn billing-only configuration with no credit balances or reservation calls.
Before committing to production

The production wallet and operating model

  1. Build Pagos’s wallet and SDK. Prove concurrent spending, reservation extension, failure cleanup, reset/expiry and rate-version behavior.
  2. Harden the integration. Durable jobs/events, duplicate protection, reconciliation and recovery across service failures.
  3. Agree the policies. Grants after discounts/proration, failed renewals, refund clawbacks and the cutoff for late usage.
  4. Qualify operations. Capacity, backups/restores, production queues/timers, payment-page authentication and actual emails.
  5. Migrate gradually. Reconcile existing balances and plans, shadow one product and verify totals before switching collection.

Sandbox success demonstrates functional feasibility. It does not establish production scalability, full disaster recovery or a completed Pagos implementation. Cash collected is not automatically the number of credits to grant; the grant comes from the approved product policy.
